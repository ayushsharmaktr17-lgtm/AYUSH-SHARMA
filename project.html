Urban Heatwave Risk in Indian Cities

Hook and Problem

n May 2024, the thermometer in Delhi hit a staggering 52.9°C (127.2°F), while national heatwave casualties climbed into the hundreds and local hospitals opened specialized "heatstroke clinics" to handle the influx of critical patients. The Problem Heatwaves across India are no longer just seasonal discomforts; they have evolved into a severe, systemic crisis. Fueled by climate change, these extreme weather events are becoming longer, more frequent, and more deadly. The danger is most acute in dense urban centers, where concrete, asphalt, and a lack of green cover create "urban heat islands" that trap oppressive temperatures day and night, disproportionately threatening vulnerable populations. Why Mapping Matters You cannot protect people if you do not know exactly where the risk is highest. Heat risk mapping is the critical tool that transforms invisible environmental danger into actionable data. By layering temperature data with demographic maps, cities can pinpoint the exact neighborhoods, informal settlements, and outdoor work zones facing the greatest threat. This precise mapping allows municipal authorities to deploy targeted emergency interventions—such as cooling centers, hydration stations, and green infrastructure—directly to the communities that need them most.

We map heatwave risk across four distinct Indian cities—Delhi, Ahmedabad, Nagpur, and Jaipur—because each represents a unique urban topography, climate micro-region, and vulnerability profile. By analyzing this specific mix of cities, our framework ensures that the satellite and demographic models can scale effectively across the entire Indian subcontinent.

Heatwave Risk

1. The Hazard represents the physical intensity, frequency, and duration of the climate event itself. It answers the question: How extreme is the environment? • Land surface temperature and ambient air temperature • Heat index (which combines air temperature and relative humidity to measure how hot it actually feels) • Number of heatwave days and the frequency of consecutive extreme heat events

2. Exposure (The Spatial Overlap) Exposure refers to the presence of people, infrastructure, or assets in the places where the hazard occurs. It answers the question: Who or what is in harm's way? • Population density (highly crowded urban centers place more lives in immediate contact with the heat) • Outdoor workers (agricultural laborers, construction crews, and street vendors who cannot escape the elements) • Slums and informal settlements (neighborhoods built in high-density urban heat islands)

3. Vulnerability (The Capacity to Cope) Vulnerability is the predisposition of the exposed population to suffer harm due to socio-economic, physiological, or structural disadvantages. It answers the question: Why are they susceptible to injury or illness? • Elderly individuals and children (demographics with reduced physiological capacity to regulate body temperature) • Socio-economic poverty (lacking financial resources to alter behaviors or pay for emergency needs) • Lack of AC or healthcare access (structural absence of cooling technology or nearby emergency medical facilities) • Low green cover neighborhoods lacking tree can

Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability

Critical Impacts & Dangers

Heatwaves are often called "silent killers" because they lack the visible violence of tornados or floods, yet they historically cause higher mortality rates.

Human Health: Extreme heat strains the cardiovascular system. When ambient temperatures reach "wet-bulb" thresholds (where extreme heat meets extreme humidity), the human body loses its physical capacity to cool itself down through sweating. This leads to severe heat exhaustion, hyperthermia, and fatal heat strokes.

The Urban Heat Island (UHI) Effect: Cities feel heatwaves much worse than rural areas. Concrete, brick, and asphalt absorb immense heat during the day and slowly radiate it back out at night, denying urban populations any overnight cooling.

• Infrastructure & Grid Failures: Surging demand for air conditioning heavily strains electricity grids, leading to blackouts. Extreme heat can also buckle railway tracks, warp asphalt roads, and cause power transformers to overheat.

• Agriculture & Ecosystems: Crops wither under heat stress, compromising food supply chains. Concurrently, prolonged dry heat turns forests and grasslands into tinderboxes, rapidly escalating the risk of explosive wildfires.

Essential Safety & Prevention Guidelines

When a heatwave warning is issued by local authorities, following these core survival measures can mitigate health risks:

Hydrate Constantly: Drink plenty of fluids (water, oral rehydration solutions, or clear broths) even if you do not feel actively thirsty. Avoid caffeine, alcohol, and heavily sugared drinks, which accelerate dehydration.

Dress for the Climate: Wear loose-fitting, lightweight, light-colored, and breathable cotton clothing. Light colors reflect solar heat rather than absorbing it.

Time Your Day: Restrict strenuous outdoor activities or physical labor to the coolest parts of the day—typically before 7:00 AM or after 6:00 PM. If traveling outside is mandatory, seek shade and carry an umbrella or a wide-brimmed hat.

• Secure the Living Space: Keep curtains, drapes, and sunshades closed over windows that receive direct sunlight during the day. Utilize fans, air coolers, or air conditioning to keep indoor spaces ventilated.

• Never Leave Anyone in Cars: Even with windows cracked, a parked car can reach lethal internal temperatures within minutes. Never leave children, vulnerable individuals, or pets unattended in a vehicle.

But heatwaves are so much more dynamic and complex that traditional methods of reporting them, purely based on absolute air temperatures, might be insufficient. Heat has a dynamism spatially, temporally, and in its interaction with materials. Hence its measurement must also be dynamic. And not just the heatwave but its effects have several dimensions as well, with respect to human health, economic activity etc. It is, therefore, necessary while measuring a heatwave to use various metrics, indices, and methodologies to get better insights into the spatiotemporal nature of heat.

protect yourself and your family during a heat wave

1. Stay Hydrated • Drink water regularly: Drink water consistently throughout the day, even before you feel thirsty. Aim for at least 2–3 liters daily. • Use ORS or electrolytes: Carry a water bottle infused with ORS, glucose, or electrolytes to replace lost salts. • Avoid dehydrating drinks: Skip alcohol, coffee, tea, and sugary or carbonated soft drinks, as they increase fluid loss. • Eat light foods: Choose water-rich foods like watermelon, cucumber, and citrus fruits; avoid heavy, high-calorie, or hot meals.

2. Keep Your Body Cool • Wear proper clothing: Choose light-colored, loose-fitting, and porous cotton clothes. • Shield yourself outdoors: Use an umbrella, a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses, and sunscreen whenever you go outside. • Avoid peak hours: Stay indoors or in the shade during the hottest part of the day, typically between 12:00 PM and 4:00 PM (or 11:00 AM – 3:00 PM depending on local guidelines). • Cool your skin: Take cool (not freezing) showers or baths, or place a damp cloth, ice pack, or wet towel on your neck, face, and pulse points.

3. Keep Your Home Cool • Block out sunlight: Close windows, blinds, and heavy curtains during the day to keep direct heat outside. • Ventilate at night: Open windows after dark or early in the morning when the outdoor air drops in temperature. • Use fans smartly: Electric fans help circulate air when indoor temperatures are under 40°C (104°F). If it is hotter than 40°C, a fan can blow hot air onto your body and increase heat strain. Place a bowl of ice or frozen water bottles in front of a fan for a DIY cooler effect. • Avoid indoor heat sources: Turn off unnecessary electrical appliances and avoid using the oven or stove. If your home becomes dangerously hot, visit public air-conditioned spaces like libraries, malls, or community centers

4. Protect Vulnerable Groups • Watch high-risk individuals: Regularly check on infants, children, pregnant women, the elderly (over 65), and people with chronic heart, lung, or kidney conditions. • Never leave anyone in a car: Never leave children, the elderly, or pets inside a parked vehicle, even for a few minutes, as interior temperatures spike to fatal levels almost instantly. • Protect babies: Do not cover infant strollers with a dry blanket (it traps heat); use a damp, thin cloth and a portable fan instead.

5. Recognize Severe Symptoms (Emergency Warning Signs) • Heat Exhaustion: Fatigue, weakness, heavy sweating, dizziness, headache, nausea, and muscle cramps. • Action: Move the person to a shaded or cool place, loosen tight clothing, and offer sips of water or ORS. • Heat Stroke (Medical Emergency): High body fever over 40°C (104°F), confusion, delirium, seizures, fainting, rapid breathing, or lack of urination. • Action: Call emergency services immediately, move the person to the shade, fan them vigorously, and apply wet cloths or ice packs. Do not force them to drink if they are unconscious.
